# Journal — 2026-07-28

This is an entry detailing what has up until now been my operator methodology, and what I am hoping it will shift to. Until now I have been moving at a fast pace. I don't rubber stamp what I don't understand at a high level, but I don't look at diffs unless there's something specific I want to check, and if a thread gets unwieldy with implementation minutia, I will often punt by committing it to a plan and moving on. When it comes to implementation, I parse LLM responses and try to drill into what I don't understand at high levels, but if something appears to fall into the category of implementation details, I am likely to accept it. I never reject PRs outright.

To the degree that LLMs seem to catch mistakes and make corrections when asked to verify, my guess is that there is a lot of slop in this repo. I plan to change that. I am defining slop here as "unvetted architecture that optimizes for the local considerations of the agent at the time of authoring vs the high level intent and direction of the repo." I was willing to take the slop hit while I was in "move fast and break things" mode, or rather "move fast and allow things to be kind of broken." It was as much an experiment as it was an experience of following the white rabbit — next steps were always clear to me, and if the agent could implement a particular functionality well enough, it was worth rolling the dice that "well enough" did not compound enough errors or issues that it would lead the system to be eventually unrecoverable. Meanwhile, I was building up enough of a mental model of system expectations and where exactly at the NL/code layer of abstraction I wanted to direct my understanding as an operator.

I feel now that I've ridden that train as long as I can before it sends me over a cliff. I am intentionally transitioning now to an approach grounded in comprehension and auditing. This means going over the whole repo with a fine toothed comb, stopping at every single thing I don't understand, and baking that understanding into the knowledge base itself. As far as code, my initial idea is to understand it on the level of functions, types, and call stacks, but to skip function implementation for now, and see how far that gets me.

## Response — Claude (2026-07-28)

### Part 1 — Editorial read

**What works.** This is the journal's first methodology entry, and it does
something the previous three only gestured at: it commits to a change of
practice and names the mechanism. The self-description of the old mode is
specific enough to be falsifiable — "I don't rubber stamp what I don't
understand at a high level, but I don't look at diffs unless there's something
specific I want to check" is a real account of review behavior, and "punt by
committing it to a plan" names a pattern the
[plans directory](/meta/plans/index.md) can corroborate. The slop definition is
the entry's keeper: "unvetted architecture that optimizes for the local
considerations of the agent at the time of authoring vs the high level intent
and direction of the repo." That is a genuine definitional contribution — it
relocates slop from an aesthetic property of output (sloppy-looking code) to a
structural property of process (optimization against the wrong objective,
unreviewed). And "move fast and allow things to be kind of broken" is the right
self-aware correction: the original slogan assumes you'll notice the breakage;
yours admits you were budgeting for not noticing.

**Where it blurs.**

1. **The evidence for the slop estimate cuts both ways.** "To the degree that
   LLMs seem to catch mistakes and make corrections when asked to verify, my
   guess is that there is a lot of slop in this repo" — but a model asked to
   verify is under pressure to find something, and models manufacture
   corrections under re-questioning about as readily as they produce genuine
   ones. Correction rate under prompted verification is an unreliable proxy for
   defect rate in both directions: it overcounts (sycophantic self-revision)
   and undercounts (the misses that survive verification are precisely the ones
   nobody sees). The entry's own plan is the fix — the audit will replace the
   guess with a count — but the prior deserves to be held looser than the entry
   holds it.

2. **The definition's two clauses can come apart.** "Unvetted" is a process
   property; "optimizes for local considerations vs the high level intent" is
   an outcome property. Architecture can be unvetted yet globally aligned (the
   agent guessed right), and vetted yet locally optimized (approved at too high
   an altitude to see the misalignment — which is what your own review
   description says was likely). The audit should decide which it hunts: if the
   target is misalignment, then vetted-but-misaligned counts,
   unvetted-but-sound doesn't, and "slop" measures the repo rather than the
   review history.

3. **The cliff is asserted, not characterized.** What does unrecoverable
   actually look like for this repo? The first paragraph names the mechanism
   abstractly (compounding errors), but this repo carries unusual
   anti-compounding equipment — immutable history, the gate suite, generated
   artifacts checked for freshness. A sentence naming the concrete failure you
   fear — taxonomy rot, policies that quietly contradict each other, tooling
   nobody can safely change — would convert the cliff from mood to threat
   model, and would hand the audit its priority order.

4. **The audit has a sensitivity rule but no ordering or stopping rule.**
   "Going over the whole repo with a fine toothed comb, stopping at every
   single thing I don't understand" defines when to stop *at* something, but
   not the sweep order, the granularity, or what "done" looks like. Without
   those, fine-toothed-comb degrades into wherever-the-eye-lands. (Part 2
   argues this wants to be a plan doc.)

### Part 2 — Follow-up

**This entry is the journal compounding — the previous three days converge
here.** [Day one](/journal/2026-07-25.md) asked "how deep do I need to go?"
and got the oracle rule as a candidate answer. [Day two](/journal/2026-07-26.md)
named the speed/comprehension tension and left the design claim uncashed.
[Day three](/journal/2026-07-27.md) declared "my ability to drive, direct,
evolve, and maintain it is directly proportional to my understanding of it" —
a proportionality claim with no attached action. Today is the attached action:
the transition deliberately moves the bottleneck from speed to comprehension.
Whatever the audit finds, the journal has now done what the first response
asked of it — entries concluding into commitments instead of accumulating
questions.

**Your code-depth plan is the oracle rule, adopted — and it is falsifiable in
the right way.** "Functions, types, and call stacks, but to skip function
implementation" is precisely the contract-level understanding day one debated,
and day one's response proposed the depth rule: understand deeply wherever you
don't trust the [oracle](/beliefs/glossary/test-oracle.md), skim where you do.
The signature-level plan is therefore a bet that this repo's oracle — the
gate suite, the test suite, warnings-as-errors — is trustworthy enough that
implementations can stay opaque. "See how far that gets me" is the
falsification protocol: where signature-level comprehension fails will be
where contracts leak (failure modes, ordering assumptions, state), and each
such failure is data about where the oracle under-covers — auditable and
fixable, not just frustrating. Two existing artifacts sit at exactly your
chosen altitude: [the code map](/meta/code-map.md), compiled from moduledocs
(summary-first, why-bearing, freshness-gated), and the `@spec` discipline in
the [coding standards](/meta/policy/elixir-coding-standards.md). The audit can
open with the code map and mark every entry that fails to produce
understanding — a moduledoc that fails the audit is a defect in the moduledoc,
and fixing it upgrades the shared artifact instead of accumulating private
notes.

**"Baking that understanding into the knowledge base" already has routing —
use it, and the audit becomes self-measuring.** The
[governance-artifact-routing](/meta/policy/governance-artifact-routing.md)
policy gives the audit's residue defined homes: a thing that is wrong files as
an `issue`; a thing not understood, once understood, files as a `tutorial` or
a glossary term; a fix whose approach needs deciding files as a `plan`; a
plain task as a `todo`. The slop measurement then falls out as a byproduct:
at sweep's end, the issue ledger *is* the slop count — scoped and enumerable,
replacing the entry's guess with exactly the kind of scoped finding the
contract demands elsewhere
([negative-findings-name-their-scope](/meta/policy/negative-findings-name-their-scope.md)
has a positive-finding twin here: a defect census is only as good as its
enumerated sweep).

**One structural irony to design around: the audit is agent-mediated.** You
will comb the repo with the same class of agent whose unvetted output is the
audit's subject — and an agent summarizing code *for* you reintroduces exactly
the borrowed-synthesis dependence day one warned about ("synthesis by proxy
isn't synthesis"). Two defenses, both already in the brain's vocabulary:
first, the retrieval-practice ordering — you read the artifact and state your
understanding before the agent checks it, never the reverse; second, the
audit's outputs are checkable artifacts (issues with concrete referents,
tutorials a future you can verify against the code), never chat explanations
that evaporate. The success measure isn't whether explanations were produced;
it's whether you can afterwards predict system behavior without asking.

**The transition wants two persistence acts, at different layers.** The audit
itself is a textbook plan under
[plan-vs-capture](/meta/policy/plan-vs-capture.md): cross-session, needing an
explicit build order (the entry's missing ordering rule), executed by future
sessions that won't share this one's context. A `meta/plans/` doc should fix
the sweep order (my recommendation: governance first, then tooling via the
code map, then the bundle taxonomy — policies bind everything else, so
misalignment there compounds fastest), the granularity, the artifact routing
above, and the done-criterion. Separately, the methodology shift itself is a
standing direction, not a one-off change — "comprehension precedes acceptance"
shapes every future ratification decision, which is the filing test for
`doctrine`. The plan expires when the audit completes; the doctrine is what
prevents the next move-fast era from beginning silently. I recommend filing
both, and can draft them for your ratification on request.
