# Journal — 2026-07-25

Today, I have rediscovered the
[Manus article](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)
from July 2025, which detailed the architecture and approach and specifically why
they opted for markdown files for their memory store. This led to a spike in
investigating
[other articles detailing the same usage](/knowledge/SWE/agentic/context-engineering/ai-agent-memory-management-markdown-files.md).
At this point, it seems to be a generally good practice and the place people are
reaching before RAG or bespoke database-driven solutions for memory. But there's
still the question of at what point do you cross over into needing a
database-driven solution? There's the question of hybrids, and there's the
question of can you buy more time using SQLite layered over a markdown-driven
memory layer? This knowledge base is far from needing anything like a
database-driven solution, and there's far too much advantage in having a
persistent immutable history to trade for a new independent layer of truth with
its own history mechanism, separate from the repo itself. But it's something to
keep in mind as this knowledge store grows, and I want to learn and better
understand the trade-offs between the systems by having actually applied them. I
don't want to follow a pattern of creating contrived usages here, but I do want
to have enough of an understanding to know when a line has been crossed and a
database should be considered.

Another aspect of the Manus article I found enlightening was the fact that Manus
was persistently writing todos to keep the overall direction of a plan top of
mind in the agent, and to avoid general goal-following suffering from the
lost-in-the-middle problem, or the issue of it being stale data — instructions
stated at the beginning of the thread. In a lot of ways, I feel like what I'm
trying to build here is in many ways a proto-Manus. Could be the epistemic
layers and knowledge systems for agents are all essentially the same thing, or
serving the same function, even if they have different approaches. Led me to
think that it's probably worth building or emulating something similar to what
Manus built with their todo system — a mechanism that provides a constant
focusing of intention and objective for the agent. One question I should
investigate is: does Claude already effectively do this, and would I be
re-engineering something that's become standard in harness architecture, or is
there a way to have control and optimize that could be potentially useful?

One of the reasons I am beginning to keep this journal is I'm finding that
strictly consuming information — which seems to be a byproduct of this new
agentic way of working, when the agent is so effective at sourcing, surfacing,
summarizing, and presenting — leads to a dependence on the agent to present and
synthesize information for the user. Which is, of course, an impossibility. I,
as the user, need to engage intellectually more, and engage more intentionally
in synthesizing from my own memory what I'm working on, what the implications
are, where the project is heading, and what questions are outstanding.

With regards to software development and code understanding, I found a fair
number of recent resources which debate the necessity to understand the code.
And it's clear that this is still an open question throughout the industry.
However, people have strong opinions no matter where they find themselves
sitting. One thing I feel myself is I can't really have an informed opinion
until I've experienced both sides of the fence. And the only way to do that is
to understand the code. The question is how deep do I need to go? If you have a
service and you treat that like a pure function, you understand the inputs. You
understand the expected outputs or the range. You understand the types. You
understand what the contract is. Do you need to know the technical
implementation? One of the advantages of functional programming is arguably you
do not. As long as the function exhibits consistent behavior in line with the
contract, you should be able to count on that behavior. And, theoretically,
enough tests should prove the integrity of the function. So what I'm doing now,
and trying to do with this repo, is get closer to an understanding of what this
intermediary layer of abstraction really is. What it really looks like — is it
pseudocode? Is it a list of invariants? Or does it by necessity need to be the
code itself? Is code an abstraction level that cannot be raised, given that
prose and natural language are essentially subjective and lead to
nondeterministic behavior by models?
