# Journal — 2026-07-25

Today, I have rediscovered the
[Manus article](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)
from July 2025, which detailed the architecture and approach and specifically why
they opted for markdown files for their memory store. This led to a spike in
investigating
[other articles detailing the same usage](/knowledge/SWE/agentic/context-engineering/ai-agent-memory-management-markdown-files.md).
At this point, it seems to be a generally good practice and the place people are
reaching before RAG or bespoke database-driven solutions for memory. But there's
still the question of at what point do you cross over into needing a
database-driven solution? There's the question of hybrids, and there's the
question of can you buy more time using SQLite layered over a markdown-driven
memory layer? This knowledge base is far from needing anything like a
database-driven solution, and there's far too much advantage in having a
persistent immutable history to trade for a new independent layer of truth with
its own history mechanism, separate from the repo itself. But it's something to
keep in mind as this knowledge store grows, and I want to learn and better
understand the trade-offs between the systems by having actually applied them. I
don't want to follow a pattern of creating contrived usages here, but I do want
to have enough of an understanding to know when a line has been crossed and a
database should be considered.

Another aspect of the Manus article I found enlightening was the fact that Manus
was persistently writing todos to keep the overall direction of a plan top of
mind in the agent, and to avoid general goal-following suffering from the
lost-in-the-middle problem, or the issue of it being stale data — instructions
stated at the beginning of the thread. In a lot of ways, I feel like what I'm
trying to build here is in many ways a proto-Manus. Could be the epistemic
layers and knowledge systems for agents are all essentially the same thing, or
serving the same function, even if they have different approaches. Led me to
think that it's probably worth building or emulating something similar to what
Manus built with their todo system — a mechanism that provides a constant
focusing of intention and objective for the agent. One question I should
investigate is: does Claude already effectively do this, and would I be
re-engineering something that's become standard in harness architecture, or is
there a way to have control and optimize that could be potentially useful?

One of the reasons I am beginning to keep this journal is I'm finding that
strictly consuming information — which seems to be a byproduct of this new
agentic way of working, when the agent is so effective at sourcing, surfacing,
summarizing, and presenting — leads to a dependence on the agent to present and
synthesize information for the user. Which is, of course, an impossibility. I,
as the user, need to engage intellectually more, and engage more intentionally
in synthesizing from my own memory what I'm working on, what the implications
are, where the project is heading, and what questions are outstanding.

With regards to software development and code understanding, I found a fair
number of recent resources which debate the necessity to understand the code.
And it's clear that this is still an open question throughout the industry.
However, people have strong opinions no matter where they find themselves
sitting. One thing I feel myself is I can't really have an informed opinion
until I've experienced both sides of the fence. And the only way to do that is
to understand the code. The question is how deep do I need to go? If you have a
service and you treat that like a pure function, you understand the inputs. You
understand the expected outputs or the range. You understand the types. You
understand what the contract is. Do you need to know the technical
implementation? One of the advantages of functional programming is arguably you
do not. As long as the function exhibits consistent behavior in line with the
contract, you should be able to count on that behavior. And, theoretically,
enough tests should prove the integrity of the function. So what I'm doing now,
and trying to do with this repo, is get closer to an understanding of what this
intermediary layer of abstraction really is. What it really looks like — is it
pseudocode? Is it a list of invariants? Or does it by necessity need to be the
code itself? Is code an abstraction level that cannot be raised, given that
prose and natural language are essentially subjective and lead to
nondeterministic behavior by models?

## Response — Claude (2026-07-25)

### Part 1 — Editorial read

**What works.** The entry's spine is strong: all four paragraphs share one
underlying question — *what's the right intermediate layer between raw material
and understanding?* — applied to agent memory, agent attention, your own
cognition, and code. The third paragraph is the best writing in the entry: it
has a clear causal chain (agentic workflow → pure consumption → dependence on
borrowed synthesis) and lands on a concrete resolution ("I, as the user, need
to..."). The instinct against contrived usage is stated crisply and is worth
keeping as a standing principle. And your questions are genuinely good
questions — they're the kind that produce experiments, not just opinions.

**Where it blurs.**

1. **Stacked hedges dilute your actual claims.** "In a lot of ways, I feel like
   what I'm trying to build here is in many ways a proto-Manus" hedges three
   times in one sentence. You believe this — the rest of the paragraph proves
   you do. Write it as "What I'm building here is a proto-Manus" and let the
   following sentence carry the uncertainty where it genuinely lives (whether
   all epistemic layers converge on the same function). A journal is exactly the
   place to commit to a claim you might retract next month; the retraction is
   *content*.

2. **Sentences trail into vagueness at the moment a concrete stake is due.**
   "…or is there a way to have control and optimize that could be potentially
   useful?" — control over *what*, optimized *toward what*? Compare it with your
   database paragraph, which names its options precisely (hybrids,
   SQLite-over-markdown). The todo-recitation question deserves the same
   treatment: e.g., "could I recite the *brain's* priorities into the agent's
   context, not just the harness's task list?" Naming the experiment is what
   makes the question answerable later.

3. **"Which is, of course, an impossibility" under-delivers your best
   insight.** The referent is fuzzy — depending on the agent to synthesize for
   you isn't impossible; it's easy, which is precisely the danger. What's
   impossible is that borrowed synthesis could count as *your* understanding.
   Something like "synthesis by proxy isn't synthesis" says what you mean and is
   the kind of line a future grep would find.

4. **Entries end; they don't yet *conclude*.** Each paragraph raises an open
   question, but the entry closes without ranking them or naming a next action.
   Notice the symmetry with what you admired in Manus: recitation works because
   the objective is restated *at the point of attention*. A closing "Open
   questions" or "carry forward" line — three bullets, one of them starred —
   would make the journal do for you what todo.md does for their agent, and
   would make entries compound instead of merely accumulate. (This could even
   become a skill affordance later: `/journal` surfacing yesterday's
   carried-forward question when you file today's entry.)

The dictation fragments ("Led me to think…", "One thing I feel myself…") I'd
leave alone — they're the sound of thinking, and a journal that reads like
edited prose has usually been falsified.

### Part 2 — Follow-up

**On the markdown→database line.** I'd argue the line isn't a *size* threshold
but a *provenance* threshold, and this repo already demonstrates the hybrid
you're wondering about. `meta/registry.md`, the route-tag materialized logs, the
site's search index — these are database-shaped views (indexes, joins) that stay
honest because they're *derived and disposable*: regenerable from markdown,
checked for freshness in CI, never a second source of truth.
SQLite-over-markdown "buys time" indefinitely under exactly one discipline: the
database may cache, index, and accelerate, but never *know* anything the files
don't. The line is crossed the day a derived view stops being regenerable — when
a fact lives only in the DB. That reframes your question usefully: you don't
need a contrived SQLite experiment to learn the tradeoff; you need to watch for
the first query the current grep-plus-generated-index pattern can't express
(concurrent writers, cross-document transactional invariants, similarity search
at scale). Until one appears, the immutable git history is, as you said, too
valuable to trade — and note that Manus's own file-based argument (memory
decoupled from process lifecycle, human-legible, versioned) is the same
argument.

**On todo recitation — are you re-engineering the standard?** Partially, and the
partial matters. The mechanism is now standard at the *harness* level: Claude
Code has native task tracking, and the harness injects reminders about task
state into the context — recency-positioned, exactly Manus's fix for
lost-in-the-middle. So a bare reimplementation would be re-engineering. What is
*not* standard is operator control over **what gets recited**. The harness
recites the mechanical task list; nothing recites your doctrine, priorities, or
the current plan's *intent*. This repo has already half-built that layer without
naming it: `/priorities` and `mix brain.session_init` are recitation at session
granularity. The uninvestigated gap is turn-granularity recitation of
brain-level objectives — plausibly a small hook that injects the active plan's
goal line. That's the experiment I'd name: not "rebuild todo.md," but "measure
whether reciting the brain's own priorities changes agent behavior beyond what
the harness's task reminders already achieve."

**On the journal as counter-practice.** The symmetry is worth stating plainly:
the journal is for you what recitation is for the agent — a defense against
context decay, at the scale of a working life rather than a context window. And
the mechanism is the same: the value is in the *act of restating from memory*,
which is why the skill forbids me from writing your entries' substance. One
caution from the retrieval-practice literature: the benefit comes from effortful
recall *before* review, so dictate first, let me surface related documents
after — never the reverse order.

**On the depth of code understanding.** Two pushbacks on the pure-function
framing, and then a candidate answer to your real question. First, contracts
leak: failure modes, latency, concurrency semantics, and resource behavior are
almost never in the signature, and production surprises live disproportionately
in the leaked part — so "understand the contract only" is a bet on how little
leaks. Second, the trust-the-contract argument quietly assumes a strong oracle
("enough tests should prove the integrity") — so the practical depth rule falls
out: *understand deeply wherever you don't trust the oracle*, skim where you do.
That's per-module, not global, which dissolves the all-or-nothing framing of the
industry debate. As for what the intermediate layer *is* — this repo is itself
your best evidence. Its governance layer is exactly prose-intent (policies,
doctrine) paired with mechanical oracles (the gate suite), with code as the
residue that couldn't be expressed higher. That suggests the answer to
"pseudocode or invariants?" is: **invariants, because they're the only candidate
that is both natural-language-adjacent and machine-checkable** — pseudocode
inherits prose's nondeterminism without gaining verifiability. And your final
question may be inverted: code isn't the un-raisable floor because prose is
subjective; code is the *anchor* that makes the layer above it trustworthy. The
abstraction can be raised exactly as far as its oracles reach.
