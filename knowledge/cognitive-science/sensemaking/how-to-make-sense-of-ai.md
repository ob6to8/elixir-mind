# How to Make Sense of AI

Cedric Chin's method for orienting to a hype-saturated, fast-changing
technology, built from roughly 1.5 years of reading histories of prior
technology bubbles (railways, oil, steel, the PC boom). Two ground rules
precede the method: attention is a limited resource — "at its core,
sensemaking is the art of regulating attention. This is a fancier way of
saying that *you must know what to ignore*" — and every act of consumption
should pass through Outcome Orientation: "at all times, whenever you are
doing something or reading something, you should ask yourself the question:
'What is the outcome I am trying to achieve here?'"

## The method

Three moves, stated directly: "You must ignore all opinions, analysis,
predictions, fanciful essays 'from the future', 'situational awareness
updates' and scenario forecasts about AI... You will pay attention only to
**detailed field reports of use**... Whilst paying attention to detailed
field reports of use you are looking to answer the **four questions of
uncertainty**": (a) what new outcomes does this field report suggest are
possible; (b) what actions could I take in response; (c) what is the
relative value of those outcomes to me specifically (given who I am, what my
company does, my goals); (d) what are the causal relationships at work. The
four questions come from consultant Vaughn Tan's *Not Knowing* framework.

Chin illustrates with concrete field reports — Microsoft's Sam Schillace
reporting teams shipping working software with zero human-written code after
roughly six months of harness-building, OpenAI's own harness-engineering
writeup treated skeptically given the vendor's incentive, and a second
Schillace report of multi-day autonomous builds — each mined for the four
questions rather than for "AI is coming for your job"-style extrapolation.

## Why this works

The underlying claim: "effective businesspeople do not 'predict the future'
more successfully than less effective businesspeople" — prediction is too
expensive and unreliable, so the better skill is "fast adaptation under
uncertainty." Forecasters want to be right; operators "just want to win,"
which favors an organization built for experimentation and rapid redirection
over one built to have called the outcome correctly in advance. "You don't
have to predict the future if you can see the present moment clearly."

Ignoring opinion pieces specifically (rather than just deprioritizing them)
is deliberate: "when a new technology emerges, impacts are uneven and
ultimately unpredictable" — no one predicted taxi drivers would be
threatened by the early internet — so analysis written from a different
vantage in the economy carries little information about *your* specific
situation; and the attention economy has a structural incentive to produce
viral, fear-tuned takes that serve "the author's benefit, not yours." The
practical test for anything read: "which of the four questions does this
piece of content answer for me? If the answer is 'none of them', then you
should ask yourself: 'why am I still reading it?'"
