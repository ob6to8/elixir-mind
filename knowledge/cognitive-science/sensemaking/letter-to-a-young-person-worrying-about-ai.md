# Letter to a Young Person Worrying About AI

Written as a reply to a Commoncog member worried AI would make young
knowledge workers obsolete. Chin's core move is to reject the premise that
avoiding harm requires accurate prediction: "you should learn to act without
the need for narrow, accurate prediction" — a lesson he draws from the
unpredictability of the Covid years: "prediction is hard and that humans are
not smart enough; I shouldn't bother with it." He names the young person's
implicit model explicitly in order to attack it: a new technology "changes
society in breakneck time," so "if you do not act before it changes society,
you will be too late," which forces prediction rather than observation.

## The corrective research project

Rather than debate any specific AI capability claim, Chin prescribes
calibration against history: read at least ten real accounts of technology
displacing workers — "have a bias towards books, with oral histories or
actual interviews with the workers being displaced" — spanning both fast and
slow cases, across industries. His own reading turned up "shocking factoids"
from David Edgerton's *The Shock of the Old*: more horses were deployed in
WWII than in any prior war, including Napoleon's campaigns; peak horse-use in
Finland's lumber industry was 1950; railroad companies still owned three to
five times more horses than motor vehicles a decade after the automobile's
invention; steam-powered ships were still being built in the 1920s; rickshaws
spread across Asia in the same decade as the Space Race; and the death of the
printed book has been predicted, wrongly, across the PC, internet, and mobile
revolutions alike.

Edgerton's diagnosis for why this surprises us: museums and documentaries
show "a history of inventions, not a history of use," so the folk model is
invention → rapid universal adoption → the old technology dies. The actual
mechanism is that "technology is never used in a vacuum. It always exists in
a sociotechnical system" — maintenance know-how, training, regulation,
unions, supply chains — and "in order to change to a new technology, you also
have to switch over all the other things around that technology. And that
takes time." Chin's stated general finding from the research: "in the vast,
vast majority of cases, it takes a long time for technological change to
destroy jobs" — and even in the fastest historical cases, there was enough
time to observe the change underway and adapt in response to it, rather than
needing to have predicted it in advance.

## The stance this supports

"It is actually a more prudent strategy to wait, watch, and then act in
response to real world, observable changes instead of speculation" — the same
"fast adaptation under uncertainty" posture as
[how to make sense of AI](/knowledge/cognitive-science/sensemaking/how-to-make-sense-of-ai.md),
applied here to an individual career rather than a business. Chin is explicit
that hearing the conclusion secondhand doesn't substitute for doing the
calibrating reading oneself: "my telling you this does NOT remove the need to
go and do this research for yourself — you can only calibrate properly if you
fill your head with the stories and experiences of others. Otherwise you will
find that your brain will discount what I am telling you."
