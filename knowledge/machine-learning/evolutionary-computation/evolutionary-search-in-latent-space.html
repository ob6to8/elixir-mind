An explorable explanation

Evolutionary search in a diffusion model's latent space

Five small widgets, each built the way Circles, Sines, and Signals teaches the Fourier transform: state one equation, then hand you a control over it. The equations belong to a 2025 paper on steering diffusion models with genetic algorithms and evolution strategies — drag things until each claim is either confirmed or broken.

Modeled on Circles, Sines, and Signals (Jack Schaedler) Equations from arXiv:2506.00299 (Jajal et al., 2025)

01

Random noise doesn't spread out — it lives on a shell

Sample a vector z with every coordinate drawn from N(0,1). In low dimensions its length varies a lot. In high dimensions, almost every sample lands within a hair's width of the same distance from the origin — a thin spherical shell at radius √d. This is the fact the whole paper's search machinery is built around.

‖z‖ concentrates near √d, for z ~ N(0, I_d) as d grows
3,000 fresh samples of ‖z‖ redrawn on every move. Dashed line marks √d.
→Drag d from 2 to 200. The histogram doesn't just shift right — it narrows relative to its own center. That narrowing is why a diffusion model's latent noise is a geometric object with real structure, not "noise" in the loose sense.
02

Why the paper only ever rotates the noise

If a candidate has to stay on the shell, an arbitrary transform is dangerous — stretch it and it falls off. A rotation is safe by construction: it changes direction without changing length.

z' = Q(A) z_T, Q(A) orthonormal ⇒ ‖z'‖ = ‖z_T‖ for every angle
The faint outline is every point the vector visits over a full turn. In rotation mode that outline is a perfect circle; the vector never leaves it.
→Predict first: switch to "arbitrary linear map," then drag the angle. Does the vector's length stay constant? Watch the outline stop being a circle.
03

Mixing two parents never leaves the shell either

The paper's genetic algorithm breeds children by flipping an independent coin per coordinate and taking that parent's value. Both parents are shell-resident Gaussians. Does the mixing probability skew the child's distribution?

Z_i = X_i if coin=1 else Y_i, X_i,Y_i ~ N(0,1) ⇒ Z_i ~ N(0,1) for every p
Bars: 6,000 fresh children at the current p. Line: the fixed N(0,1) curve — it never moves.
→Drag p to an extreme like 5% or 95%. The bars keep hugging the same curve — this is the proof in the paper's appendix, made falsifiable: try to make the child distribution skew, and you can't.
04

Genetic algorithms narrow fast; evolution strategies don't

Both families search by generating a population and keeping what scores well. A genetic algorithm's population is discrete: crank up how hard it selects the best candidates (larger tournament size) and diversity collapses within a few generations. An evolution strategy instead nudges a whole probability distribution, and has no tournament step to crank at all — which is structurally why it stays diverse for longer, matching Figure 4 of the paper.

GA: population {ψᵢ}, tournament selection + crossover + mutation ES: q_φ = N(μ,σ), μ,σ updated from a reward-weighted average
GA population spread ES search-distribution spread
Toy 1-D landscape, 40 individuals, 60 generations — illustrates the qualitative shape of Fig. 4, not a reproduction of CoSyNE/SNES. The slider only drives the GA; ES has no tournament step, by construction.
→Push k to 15. The blue GA line collapses to near-zero within a handful of generations. The orange ES line barely notices — it was never wired to that dial.
05

"Fitness" is a dot product with an unknown good direction

Every candidate the search evaluates gets scored by a reward model. Zoomed all the way out, scoring a direction is scoring how well it agrees with some direction the reward model prefers — the same operation Σ a[n]·b[n] that Circles, Sines, and Signals uses to introduce the dot product.

reward(ψ) ∝ u · ψ, for some (unknown, reward-model-defined) direction u
Drag the pale candidate vector around the ring. The gold vector is the direction the reward model happens to favor — never shown to the real search, only to you.
→Find the angle that makes the readout most negative, then most positive. That's the entire optimization problem every algorithm on this page is solving — just in thousands of dimensions instead of two.

What's illustrative vs. exact: widgets 1–3 compute the real equations directly. Widget 4 is a simplified toy landscape chosen to show the qualitative shape of the paper's population-diversity result, not a reproduction of its actual algorithms (CoSyNE / PGPE / SNES). Widget 5 stands in for a learned reward model with a fixed linear one.
