# The End of Mathematics — AI and the collapse of research incentives

Litt's argument is not that AI can't do mathematics — it's that
mathematics-as-a-profession has reward structures built for a world of
scarce results, and those structures fail badly once results become
abundant. If AI assistance lets any researcher generate large volumes of
correct-but-shallow proofs and conjectures, and institutions keep counting
papers and prioritizing "first to claim," the dominant career strategy
becomes playing the slot machine for conjectures with AI as the lever,
rather than developing deep understanding of any one area.

## Early symptoms

He points to symptoms already visible: an explosion of arXiv submissions
whose significance is hard to triage, a *decline* in MathOverflow activity
since early 2025 (fewer people asking or answering, plausibly because AI
already answers routine questions and deep questions go unshared to protect
priority), and multiple independent groups quietly duplicating the same
AI-assisted results because nobody is discussing work-in-progress anymore.
The mechanism is a vicious cycle: institutions that don't adapt their
incentives end up rewarding people who don't engage deeply with the
mathematics, which erodes the human capital base the field depends on,
which in turn makes fewer people capable of judging what AI output is
actually significant.

## The framing

Litt frames this as exposing a pre-existing flaw — reward mechanisms (paper
counts, prestige, priority) were already misaligned with the stated values
of the field (quality, understanding) — and AI just removes the natural
rate-limiting step (scarcity of results) that used to paper over the
misalignment. This is explicitly a cautionary scenario, not a prediction.
